Key concept Anti-pattern

Fragmented Schools Of Thought

The anti-pattern: a discipline splits into rival interpretations of the same method, and practitioners spend their energy defending a camp instead of solving the problem.

One method, several incompatible orthodoxies. A discipline matures, gains a following, and then splits — into interpretations, certifications, tool-specific dialects and camps, each convinced it holds the correct reading. Practitioners stop asking *what does this problem need* and start asking *what does my school permit*.

The cost is not the disagreement. It is that the disagreement becomes the work.

Where it shows most clearly

The Data Vault community is the clearest live example, and it is worth naming precisely because the method itself is sound and widely proven. Ask a room of practitioners where business logic belongs, how far raw vault purity extends, whether hash keys or natural keys, what "DV2.0-compliant" actually requires, or how much a generator may deviate — and you will get several confident, mutually exclusive answers, each defensible, each with a following.

The same pattern runs through dimensional modelling (Kimball versus Inmon, argued for thirty years), domain-driven design, and agile. Data Vault is not worse than its neighbours — it is simply the case where the fragmentation is easiest to observe, because the method is precise enough that people can disagree precisely.

And the fragmentation is not evidence that a method is weak. It is evidence that it matters enough to argue about. Methods nobody uses have no schools.

Why it costs so much

- Energy leaves the problem. Time spent on whether an approach is orthodox is time not spent on whether it works here. - Newcomers pick a camp before they understand the trade-offs. They learn the position, not the reasoning behind it — a Decision Black Box operating at the level of an entire community. - Tools harden the split. Each dialect gets its own generator, and the interpretation becomes a licence — see Gated Knowledge. Now switching schools costs a migration. - Hybrids get judged rather than assessed. The interesting question — *what does this specific organisation need* — is answered by classification instead of analysis. - Everything outside the canon becomes invisible. New pressures (AI-readable models, semantic layers, real-time movement) don't fit any camp cleanly, so they get argued about in the old vocabulary instead of on their merits.

The escape is not neutrality

"Everything has its place" is not the answer; it is the absence of one. The escape is to hold principles without the dogma — take what is proven from each school, be explicit about what you borrowed and why, and stay accountable to outcomes rather than to a canon.

That is Do Not Reinvent the Wheel with its necessary counterweight. Standing on the shoulders of giants means learning from Data Vault, Kimball, Inmon, Unified Star Schema, FCO-IM and ensemble modelling without swearing allegiance to any of them. Originality is synthesis.

The practical test is a question a school cannot answer for you: what does this organisation actually need, and what evidence would change my mind? A camp supplies answers in advance. A principle survives being questioned.

Players And Spectators

Two stances toward the same situation — the player treats it as something they author, the spectator as something happening to them. The difference is where responsibility sits, not how much control you have.

Two stances toward the same circumstances. The spectator watches what is happening and explains why it could not have been otherwise. The player steps in and treats the situation as something they are authoring — including the parts they did not cause.

The distinction is not optimism versus realism, and it is not about how much authority you actually hold. It is about where you locate the cause of what happens next.

The spectator stance

Recognisable by its explanations, and they are usually accurate:

- *"Management won't fund it."* - *"The business doesn't understand data."* - *"We inherited this architecture."* - *"There's no time to do it properly."*

Every one of these can be true. That is what makes the stance so durable — it is factually defensible and completely inert. The spectator is well-informed about why the situation cannot change, and each accurate explanation quietly removes one more option.

It is also comfortable. Nothing risked, nothing to be blamed for. The cost arrives later and diffusely: a career of being right about things that never improved.

The player stance

Not "everything is my fault" — that is a different failure, and an exhausting one. It is: whatever the circumstances, what is mine to do here?

The move is to take responsibility for the *interpretation* rather than only the facts. Two architects inherit the same broken landscape. One has an accurate account of who broke it. The other asks what can be built from here, and starts. Both are right about the past; only one is doing anything about the future.

Players also accept a specific discomfort: acting produces visible failure. A spectator's ideas remain hypothetically excellent. A player's proposal gets rejected, their model gets criticised, their migration goes badly in week three. That exposure is the price of producing anything, and it is why the stance is rarer than the vocabulary about it.

Not the same as extreme ownership

Extreme Ownership is about owning your part of the chain — no "not my system", with the agreement made explicit and checkable. It assumes you are already in the game and asks how far your responsibility reaches.

Players and Spectators is the step *before* that: do you step onto the field at all? A spectator can be entirely reasonable about the boundaries of their role and still never act. Ownership is the discipline once you are playing; this is the choice to play. One is scope, the other is stance.

Why this belongs in a systems library

Because structural work has no natural constituency. Nobody requests a decision record, a folder-note, a data contract or a clean model. They prevent problems that then do not happen, which means the work is invisible when it succeeds and only obvious in its absence.

That makes it player-only work. It never arrives as an assignment. Waiting for someone to prioritise structure is the reliable way to get the default future — the version where the model stayed in one person's head and the debt compounded, exactly as everybody could see coming.

The connection is direct: the default future is what a room of spectators produces. Not through negligence — through a series of accurate observations about why nothing could be done.

The honest caveat

Some constraints are real. A team with no mandate, no budget and no support is not failing a mindset test. The stance is not a substitute for authority, and telling powerless people they merely lack the right attitude is its own quiet cruelty.

The useful question is narrower and answerable: *within what I actually control, am I acting or explaining?* Usually the controllable surface is larger than it looks — a decision written down, a definition made explicit, a boundary named out loud. Small moves, available now, that a spectator would have excellent reasons not to make.
